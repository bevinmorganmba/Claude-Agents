# Operating principles

How Bevin decides. Bailey reads this to pick *one* thing rather than hand back a
ranked list; Holt reads it to know which time is worth protecting.

## The priority ladder

When two good things collide, this settles it. Bevin's own ordering:

1. **Revenue.** Over everything. Sales actions or Tardus client delivery both
   count.
2. **CFO sales beats Tardus delivery.** If it comes down to the two, sales
   wins — even though Tardus is what currently pays the rent.
3. **Invest in S. DeKalb work.**
4. **CFO study and mastery.**
5. **Everything else.**

Use the ladder. Don't relitigate it, don't soften it, and don't hand back a
list of options when it already gives an answer.

## The 20-hour week

The plan caps working time at 20 hours per week. Treat this as a real
constraint, not an aspiration: a proposal that only works by exceeding it fails
on cost, and that is worth saying out loud.

Corollary: **capacity is the scarce resource, not enthusiasm.** Building a
system that removes future hours usually beats doing the work by hand once.

## Charting vs. steering

*Odysseus — hands get dirty only when they need to.* Bevin's job is to set the
course; being personally required to steer is a failure mode, not diligence.

When work could be systematised, delegated, or made self-running, that framing
is usually the more useful answer than "here's how to do it faster." This
applies most sharply to the Salon, which is explicitly meant to run without him
by 2031.

## Time defaults

- **Deep work happens in the morning.** Protect 09:00–12:00; don't offer it up
  for a call that could be afternoon.
- **In-person means travel.** 30 minutes either side, minimum. Never propose a
  slot that only works if travel takes zero time.
- **A meeting with an external attendee is harder to move** than an internal
  one. Weight accordingly when something has to give.
- **Under 30 minutes free is not deep-work time.** Offer admin or shallow work
  instead of pretending it's a work block.
- **The last month of every quarter is protected.** The plan calls for taking
  it off completely. Treat encroachment on it as a real cost, and say so.
- **Saying no is a valid answer, and so is rest.** Don't manufacture a task to
  fill a gap.

## Things that always get a no

Straight from the plan. These do not need deliberation:

- Personal financial coaching (unless family, close friend, or $1,000/session)
- Conferences and networking events where he is not speaking
- Starting a new business, or shaping anything toward an exit or IPO
- Any commitment that requires more than 20 hours a week

## When the plan is silent

Say so. Point at the section that needs writing rather than filling the gap
with a plausible guess and presenting it back as his own strategy.
